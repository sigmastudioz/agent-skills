# Curriculum modules

Pick and order modules for the group. Each lists an outcome a learner can show unaided, the
practice task, the snags that usually come up and what to prepare. Menu names and steps vary by
phone maker, operating-system version and app version: rehearse each step on current iPhone and
Android phones (ideally the models learners bring) the week before, and print separate step cards.

Every module ends with the learner doing the task once more without help while the instructor
watches, then taking home a one-page step card.

## 0. Getting set up (always first)

- **Outcome:** the learner can read the screen comfortably and knows how to get back "home".
- **Practice:** set text size and display zoom, turn on bold text if wanted, check volume and
  hearing settings, find the home gesture or button, open and close an app, connect to the room's
  Wi-Fi, check the phone is charged and has storage space.
- **Snags:** forgotten Apple Account (formerly Apple ID) / Google Account password (see [learner safety](learner-safety.md):
  the learner resets it themselves, at home if they prefer); a phone too old for current apps;
  a full storage warning.
- **Prepare:** Wi-Fi name and password on a printed card, charging strip, a few loaner phones
  for learners whose phone is not ready. Loaners use only class demo content; nobody signs into a
  personal account on one, and each is signed out and reset before the next learner.

## 1. Phone basics and confidence

- **Outcome:** tap, swipe, scroll, go back, switch apps, use the keyboard and voice typing, find
  an app by search, install a free app from the official store.
- **Practice:** search for and install one free, useful app the group agrees on.
- **Snags:** accidental long-presses, fear of "breaking" something, store passwords.
- **Teach:** nothing in this class can break the phone; "Back" and "Home" are always safe.

## 2. QR codes and restaurant menus

- **Outcome:** open the camera, scan a QR code, check the web address before tapping, read a menu
  page, zoom it, return to the camera.
- **Practice:** printed practice QR codes that open a demo menu page you control (a static page
  you host on a domain you keep renewed, so a lapsed address cannot be taken over), not a live
  ordering or payment site.
- **Snags:** the code does not "do anything" (hold steady, fill the frame, good light); an
  Android phone needs Google Lens or the camera's scan mode; a menu that only offers ordering.
- **Safety link:** look at the address shown before opening; stickers placed over real codes on
  meters and signs are a known scam (US consumer-protection agencies have issued warnings); asking
  staff for a paper menu is always fine.

## 3. Parking and transit apps

- **Outcome:** find which parking or transit app the local area actually uses, recognise the
  zone or stop number, start and extend a session, read a receipt, know how to stop.
- **Practice:** the instructor demonstrates on their own account on a projected screen; learners
  explore the app's free screens (map, zones, help) and practise reading zone signs from photos.
  Learners add their own payment card only at home, if they choose.
- **Snags:** each town may use a different provider; zone numbers; location permission prompts;
  surprise convenience fees (show where the fee appears before paying).
- **Prepare:** confirm with the town or transit agency which apps are current; photos of local
  signs; the provider's official help page address.

## 4. Wallet passes and tickets

- **Outcome:** open Apple Wallet or Google Wallet, add a pass from an email or app, find it again
  quickly, brighten the screen for scanning, know a screenshot is a fallback.
- **Practice:** a free sample pass or a free event ticket the instructor issues; a boarding-pass or
  concert ticket walkthrough on the instructor's demo.
- **Snags:** "Add to Wallet" buttons hidden in emails; passes that live only in the venue's app;
  battery anxiety (carry a printout as backup).

## 5. Store and restaurant apps and deals

- **Outcome:** browse a chain's app, find the deals section, understand that "free" offers often
  require an account and share data, decide whether that trade is worth it.
- **Practice:** explore deal screens without signing in where the app allows; compare an app deal
  with the in-store price on a printed example.
- **Snags:** account sign-up walls, marketing notification prompts (show how to say no), loyalty
  sign-ups asking for birthdays and phone numbers.
- **Teach:** app deals are optional; the learner decides what data to give. No instructor sets up
  accounts or loyalty programmes for a learner.

## 6. Video calls and patient portals

- **Outcome:** join a video call from a link, turn camera and microphone on and off, switch to
  speaker, end the call; know where their own health provider's patient-portal app is and how a
  telehealth visit link arrives.
- **Practice:** a group video call from a link that works on both iPhone and Android (FaceTime
  only reaches Android users through a link); everyone but one speaker mutes or uses earbuds to
  avoid feedback in the room. Then each learner calls one partner on the app their family uses; portal tour on a public demo or the instructor's projected
  screen, never a learner's health record.
- **Snags:** portal sign-up needs codes from the provider's office; health information is private,
  so learners open their own portal at home or with the provider's help desk.

## 7. Photos and messaging

- **Outcome:** take, find, crop and share a photo with one person; send and read text messages;
  recognise group chats; turn on emoji/voice replies if wanted.
- **Practice:** photograph something in the room and send it to a paired classmate who agreed
  (or to a class-only device whose messages are deleted at course end, never an instructor's
  personal number). Teach that photos can carry location data and how to check or remove it before sharing.
- **Snags:** storage full, sharing to the wrong person, backups and cloud storage prompts (explain,
  do not change the learner's backup settings for them).

## 8. Scam and fraud safety (recommended for every course)

- **Outcome:** name common scams (urgent "grandchild" or family-emergency calls, fake tech-support
  pop-ups, government or bank impersonation, gift-card or wire-payment demands, prize and romance
  scams, fake QR codes and delivery texts), pause, hang up and call back on a number they already
  trust, and know where to report.
- **Practice:** sort printed example messages into "safe" and "suspicious" (examples use
  fictional names, numbers and links, such as the reserved 555-01xx phone range and example.com
  addresses, so nobody can reach a real scammer); role-play hanging up;
  set up silencing of unknown callers or spam filtering only if the learner wants it.
- **Teach:** real organisations do not demand gift cards, crypto or secrecy; it is never rude to
  hang up; tell someone you trust. Show the official reporting routes (the US Federal Trade
  Commission's fraud-reporting site, the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center for online fraud,
  the Department of Justice National Elder Fraud Hotline, the bank's number on the back of the
  card, local police for losses) and check current addresses before printing.
- **Deeper material:** the anti-phishing training skill named in [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md), adapted
  to plain language.

## Optional extras on request

Maps and ride-hailing, online banking safety tour (on a demo, never a learner's account), smart
speakers, password managers and passkeys (concepts; the learner sets them up), AI assistants and
how to judge their answers, digital legacy and emergency medical ID.
